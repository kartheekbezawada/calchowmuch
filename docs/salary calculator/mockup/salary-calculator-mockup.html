Salary and take-home pay: a complete guide

Your gross salary is the number on your contract. Your take-home pay is the number that actually arrives in your bank account. The gap between them can be substantial — for a UK worker earning £60,000, roughly £14,600 disappears into income tax and National Insurance before anything reaches them, and that is before pension contributions or student loan repayments are considered. For a US worker on $100,000, the combination of federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and state income tax can absorb a comparable share.

This calculator exists to close that gap in understanding. It converts a salary between every common pay period, and — when you select a country — estimates what you will actually receive after the deductions that apply where you live and work. It also generates a pay sheet showing the specific dates you will be paid and how much will land on each one.

How to Guide

Step 1 — Choose your mode

Three modes sit at the top of the calculator. Gross Pay performs pure pay-period conversion with no tax applied at all — useful when comparing job offers quoted in different units, or when you already know your net figure and simply want it expressed weekly instead of annually. UK and USA apply that country's tax rules to estimate take-home pay.

If you are unsure which you need, start with your country. Gross Pay mode is deliberately simpler and hides every tax-related control, so nothing on screen is irrelevant to what you are doing.

Step 2 — Enter your pay and its frequency

Enter the amount you know, then select the frequency it refers to. This matters more than people expect: entering 3,000 with "Monthly" selected produces a very different calculation from entering 3,000 with "Weekly" selected. The calculator normalises whatever you enter into an annual figure internally, then works back out to every other period.

If you are paid hourly, choose Hourly and the calculator will ask for your typical hours per week and paid weeks per year so it can annualise correctly.

Step 3 — Select your location

In UK mode, choose your region. This is not cosmetic: Scotland operates its own income tax bands that differ meaningfully from those used in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, so the same salary produces a different take-home figure depending on where you are resident for tax purposes.

In USA mode, select your state. State income tax varies enormously — nine states levy none at all, several apply a single flat rate, and the remainder use graduated brackets. No state is selected by default, because assuming one would silently produce a number that is wrong for most users.

Step 4 — Add optional details

Everything beyond salary and location is optional and hidden until you ask for it. Tap any of the four chips to reveal only that section:

  • Bonus — enter the amount and the month it is typically paid. The month matters because the pay sheet will show the bonus landing on that specific payday rather than averaging it invisibly across the year.
  • Pension — enter your contribution as a percentage. Pension contributions usually reduce taxable income, so adding this changes both your tax and your take-home.
  • Student loan — select your plan. UK plans have different repayment thresholds and rates, and choosing the wrong one can shift the result by hundreds of pounds a year.
  • Pay schedule — set your first pay date and how weekend paydays are handled. This unlocks the pay sheet.

Step 5 — Read your results

The large figure is your estimated take-home pay. Below it, a single bar shows visually where your gross salary went — the green segment is what you keep, and the remaining segments are tax, National Insurance or FICA, and any pension contribution. The smaller cards translate your take-home into every pay period at once.

The effective rate shown alongside is your total deductions as a percentage of gross pay. This is almost always lower than your marginal rate — the rate applied to your next pound earned — and confusing the two is one of the most common misunderstandings about how tax works.

Step 6 — Expand the calculation

"See how this was calculated" opens a full breakdown: which tax bands were applied, how much of your income fell into each, and every deduction listed individually. Nothing is hidden behind a black box. If a number looks wrong to you, open this panel first — it will usually show you which assumption differs from your situation.

UK deductions explained UK mode

Income Tax

UK income tax is progressive: different portions of your income are taxed at different rates. Everyone starts with a Personal Allowance taxed at 0%. Income above that is taxed at the basic rate, then the higher rate, then the additional rate as you cross each threshold.

A critical detail many calculators handle poorly is the Personal Allowance taper. Once income exceeds £100,000, the allowance reduces gradually and disappears entirely at £125,140. This creates an unusually high effective marginal rate in that band — often described as a "60% trap" — because you lose allowance at the same time you pay higher-rate tax on the income causing the loss.

National Insurance

National Insurance is a separate deduction from income tax, with its own thresholds. For standard employees, contributions apply between a lower and upper earnings limit at one rate, and above the upper limit at a much lower rate. It is not a flat percentage of salary, and any calculator that applies a single percentage to your gross pay will be wrong.

Pension contributions

Workplace pension contributions typically reduce your taxable income, meaning the true cost to your take-home pay is less than the contribution amount. Contributing £3,000 as a higher-rate taxpayer may reduce your take-home by considerably less than £3,000, because part of that contribution would otherwise have gone to tax.

Student loans

UK student loan repayments are calculated as a percentage of income above a plan-specific threshold, entirely separately from income tax and National Insurance. Plans 1, 2, 4, 5 and Postgraduate each have different thresholds and rates. If you hold both an undergraduate and a postgraduate loan, both may apply simultaneously.

US deductions explained USA mode

Federal income tax

Like the UK, US federal income tax is progressive, with brackets that differ by filing status — Single, Married Filing Jointly, Married Filing Separately, or Head of Household. Your standard deduction reduces taxable income before brackets are applied, and it too varies by filing status.

FICA — Social Security and Medicare

Social Security is levied at a fixed rate up to an annual wage base, above which no further Social Security tax is due. Medicare has no wage cap, and an Additional Medicare Tax applies above a threshold. These are separate from federal income tax and are not affected by your tax bracket.

State income tax

This is where US calculations diverge most sharply. Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming levy no state income tax on wages. Several states apply a single flat rate. The rest use graduated brackets with their own deductions and exemptions, which frequently differ from federal rules.

State payroll taxes

Beyond income tax, some states levy additional payroll deductions that directly reduce take-home pay — State Disability Insurance, Paid Family and Medical Leave contributions, and in a few states an employee-side unemployment insurance contribution. These are easy to overlook but represent real money on every paycheck for workers in the affected states.

Local and city income taxes

A number of US cities and counties levy their own income tax on top of federal and state tax. New York City and Yonkers, Philadelphia, most Ohio municipalities, several Maryland counties, Detroit, St. Louis, Kansas City, Louisville and Birmingham all apply local rates to residents, non-resident workers, or both.

This calculator does not include local income tax. If you live or work in one of the jurisdictions above, your actual take-home pay will be lower than the figure shown here — for a New York City resident this difference can run to several thousand dollars a year. Treat the USA estimate as federal, FICA and state-level only.

Pay, bonuses and your pay sheet

Pay frequency explained

Pay frequency affects the size of each paycheck but not your annual total. The distinction that causes the most confusion is between bi-weekly and 4-weekly pay, which sound similar but are not:

FrequencyPeriods/yearCalculation
Weekly52Annual ÷ 52
Bi-weekly26Annual ÷ 26 — every 14 days
4-weekly13Annual ÷ 13 — every 28 days
Monthly12Annual ÷ 12

Bi-weekly pay produces 26 paychecks a year; 4-weekly produces 13. Because a year contains slightly more than 52 weeks, bi-weekly employees receive three paychecks in two months of most years — a detail worth planning around rather than treating as a windfall.

A common error worth avoiding: 4-weekly pay is not monthly pay adjusted slightly. It is the annual figure divided by 13. Calculating it as monthly × 12 ÷ 13 introduces rounding errors and misrepresents how the pay actually arrives.

How bonuses are taxed

Bonuses are taxable income, but the amount withheld from a bonus payment often differs from the eventual tax liability on it. This is why a bonus frequently appears to be taxed at a punishing rate when it arrives — payroll systems may withhold at a flat supplemental rate or treat the larger paycheck as though it represented your normal earnings.

This calculator estimates your annual position rather than replicating payroll withholding. It shows your take-home with and without the bonus, and the difference between them is your genuine net bonus. That is a more useful answer than applying your marginal rate to the bonus amount, which ignores the possibility that the bonus pushes part of your income into a higher band.

Using the pay sheet

Enabling the Pay schedule option generates a table of your upcoming paydays. Each row shows the date, gross amount, each deduction, and the net amount landing in your account. If you have entered a bonus, the payday falling in your selected bonus month is highlighted and shows the larger amount.

The pay sheet defaults to matching the pay frequency you selected above, but you can switch it independently — useful for seeing what your income would look like if your employer paid monthly instead of bi-weekly, or for budgeting against a different rhythm than you are paid on.

The weekend adjustment setting reflects a real payroll practice: when a scheduled payday falls on a Saturday or Sunday, most employers pay on the preceding Friday, though some pay on the following Monday. Selecting the correct behaviour keeps the generated dates aligned with reality.

Worked examples

Example 1 — £60,000 salary in England

A Personal Allowance of £12,570 is taxed at 0%. The next £37,700 falls in the basic-rate band at 20%, producing £7,540. The remaining £9,730 falls into the higher-rate band at 40%, producing £3,892 — a total income tax charge of £11,432. National Insurance adds approximately £3,211. With a 5% pension contribution of £3,000, estimated take-home pay is around £42,357 a year, or £3,530 a month.

Example 2 — the same salary in Scotland

Scotland applies its own bands with additional intermediate rates. The same £60,000 salary therefore produces a different take-home figure. This is precisely why the region selector is not optional in UK mode — using England's bands for a Scottish taxpayer produces a materially incorrect answer.

Example 1 — $100,000 in Texas versus California

Federal income tax and FICA are identical in both states. Texas levies no state income tax, so take-home pay is higher. California applies graduated state income tax plus State Disability Insurance. The same nominal salary therefore delivers noticeably different take-home pay depending purely on state — often several thousand dollars a year.

Gross Pay mode applies no tax at all, so there is nothing to work through band by band: £60,000 a year is £5,000 a month, £1,153.85 a week and £30.77 an hour at 37.5 hours. Switch to UK or USA above to see how tax and other deductions change those figures.

Common mistakes when comparing salaries

  • Comparing gross against net. A £55,000 offer is not worse than a £58,000 offer if the first includes materially better pension contributions. Compare like with like.
  • Ignoring the schedule. A salary quoted for 37.5 hours per week is not equivalent to the same figure for 45 hours. Convert to hourly before deciding.
  • Confusing effective and marginal rates. Entering a higher tax band does not mean all your income is taxed at that rate — only the portion above the threshold is.
  • Assuming state does not matter. In the US, two identical offers in different states can differ by thousands after tax.
  • Treating a bonus as guaranteed. Discretionary bonuses are not contractual salary. Budget on your base.

FAQ

Does this calculator show take-home pay or gross pay?

Both. Gross Pay mode converts between pay periods with no tax applied. UK and USA modes estimate take-home pay after the deductions applicable in that country.

Why does my actual payslip differ from this estimate?

Payroll systems apply withholding rules that can differ from annual tax liability, and your employer may apply benefits, salary sacrifice arrangements, or deductions not modelled here. This calculator estimates your annual position rather than replicating a specific payroll run.

Does the calculator include local or city taxes?

Where local income taxes are supported, they are shown separately in the breakdown. Where they are not available for your jurisdiction, the calculator states this clearly rather than presenting the estimate as complete.

Why is Scotland listed separately from the rest of the UK?

Scotland sets its own income tax bands and rates, which differ from those applying in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The same salary produces a different take-home figure depending on where you are resident for tax purposes.

How is my bonus taxed?

The calculator adds the bonus to your annual income, calculates total tax on the combined figure, and reports the difference between take-home with and without the bonus. This is more accurate than applying a single marginal rate to the bonus amount.

What is the difference between bi-weekly and 4-weekly pay?

Bi-weekly means every 14 days — 26 paychecks a year. 4-weekly means every 28 days — 13 paychecks a year. They are not interchangeable, and the resulting paycheck amounts differ.

Can I use this to compare two job offers?

Yes. Run each offer with its own salary, location and schedule assumptions, then compare take-home rather than gross. Offers in different countries, states, or with different working hours are frequently not comparable on gross alone.

Are my salary details stored or sent anywhere?

No. All calculations run locally in your browser - no data is stored.
