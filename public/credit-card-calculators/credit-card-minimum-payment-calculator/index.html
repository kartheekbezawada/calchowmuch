How Is a Credit Card Minimum Payment Calculated?

Most US card issuers calculate your minimum payment as the greater of a flat dollar floor or a small percentage of your balance — and most add that month's interest and any fees on top. This calculator models the percentage-plus-floor form:

Minimum payment = max(balance x minimum payment rate, lowest monthly payment)

Worked example using this page's default scenario: a balance of $3,200 at a 2.5% minimum payment rate gives $3,200 x 2.5% = $80. Because $80 is larger than the $25 floor, the required payment that month is $80. Next month the balance is lower, so the percentage-based amount is lower too — which is exactly why minimum-only payoff drags on.

How to Guide

Why Minimum Payments Slow Down Debt Repayment

As your balance declines, percentage-based minimums usually decline too. That means less of each payment goes toward principal over time, which can extend payoff and increase the overall borrowing cost.

In this scenario, payoff takes 245 months (about 20.4 years), with total interest of 6,758.05.

This is why minimum-only repayment often feels manageable month to month but expensive over the full timeline.

The Role of the Minimum Payment Floor

The lowest monthly payment floor keeps your required payment from dropping below 25.00. Without a floor, required payments could become very small near the end of the schedule and stretch payoff further.

Early in payoff, the percentage-based amount tends to control the minimum. Later, the floor often becomes the active minimum once the balance is lower.

Why Minimum-Only Repayment Becomes Expensive

Starting from a balance of 3,200.00, this minimum-only path leads to total payments of 9,958.05, which is 3.1x your starting balance.

APR of 21.9% corresponds to roughly 1.83% per month, so interest can continue compounding as long as a balance remains.

Total interest in this run is 6,758.05, and total paid is 9,958.05, which shows how long repayment windows magnify overall borrowing cost.

How Fixed Payments Change the Outcome

One practical way to reduce cost is to move from minimum-only payments to a fixed higher monthly payment. Fixed payments reduce principal faster, shorten payoff time, and lower cumulative interest.

  • Faster principal reduction month by month
  • Lower total interest over the life of the balance
  • Clearer payoff planning and less long-term repayment drag

How Credit Card Interest Works

Credit card APR is an annual rate, but interest accrues on a monthly or daily basis. With APR at 21.9%, the approximate monthly rate is 1.83%. If the balance is not paid in full, interest keeps accruing on the remaining amount.

When minimum payments are close to the interest charge, principal falls slowly and payoff duration increases.

When Minimum Payments Can Be Useful

Minimum payments can be useful temporarily when cash flow is tight and your goal is to stay current and avoid late-payment penalties while preparing a stronger repayment plan.

  • Short-term cash flow pressure
  • Protecting payment history while restructuring budget
  • Transitioning to a fixed-payment or refinance strategy

Strategies to Pay Off Faster

Moving from minimum-only payments to a fixed higher monthly payment generally lowers total interest and shortens payoff time. Even modest increases can materially improve the long-term outcome.

  • Pay more than the minimum whenever possible
  • Set a fixed monthly target to accelerate principal reduction
  • Avoid new spending on the same card during payoff

Important Assumptions in This Estimate

This estimate assumes fixed APR, no new purchases, no additional fees, and on-time monthly payments. Real credit card statements can differ if rates change, fees apply, or spending behavior changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is a credit card minimum payment?

A: A credit card minimum payment is the lowest amount your issuer requires each month to keep the account current and avoid late-payment penalties.

Q: How is the minimum payment calculated?

A: Most issuers use this formula: minimum payment = the greater of (balance x minimum payment rate) or the payment floor. For example, on a $3,200 balance with a 2.5% minimum payment rate and a $25 floor, the percentage-based amount is $3,200 x 2.5% = $80, and since $80 is higher than the $25 floor, the minimum payment is $80. This calculator applies that same formula every month as your balance changes.

Q: Why does it take so long to pay off with minimum payments?

A: Minimum payments usually decline as your balance drops, so less principal is reduced each month while interest keeps accruing.

Q: What is the minimum payment trap?

A: The minimum payment trap is when declining required payments make balances last for years, causing much higher total interest costs.

Q: How does the lowest monthly payment floor work?

A: The lowest monthly payment floor is a fixed dollar amount that sets the smallest allowed minimum payment even when the percentage-based amount becomes lower.

Q: What happens if I pay more than the minimum?

A: Paying more than the minimum reduces principal faster, shortens payoff time, and lowers total interest compared with minimum-only payments.

Q: Does the minimum payment rate vary by card issuer?

A: Yes. Issuers can use different minimum payment formulas, percentages, and lowest payment floors, so your monthly requirement can vary by card.

Q: Can my minimum payment ever go up?

A: Yes. It can increase if your balance rises, your issuer changes terms, or fees and penalty APR adjustments apply.

Q: Does this calculator include new purchases or fees?

A: No. This model assumes no new purchases and no additional fees during payoff so you can isolate the minimum-payment repayment path.

Q: How can I estimate a faster payoff strategy?

A: Compare the minimum-only outcome with a higher fixed monthly payment and use the difference in months and interest to set a faster payoff target.

Q: Does this work for Capital One, Chase, Discover, Citi, or other specific card issuers?

A: Yes. The percentage-of-balance-plus-floor formula modeled here matches how most major issuers, including Capital One, Chase, Discover, Citi, American Express, Wells Fargo, and Bank of America, structure minimum payments. Check your card's terms for the exact percentage and floor amount, then enter those values for a precise estimate.

Q: What is the minimum payment on a $5,000 credit card balance?

A: Using the same percentage-plus-floor formula, a $5,000 balance at a 2.5% minimum payment rate works out to $5,000 x 2.5% = $125, which is above a typical $25 floor, so the minimum payment would be about $125. On a smaller $1,000 balance, 2.5% of the balance is exactly $25, so the floor and the percentage amount are equal and the minimum payment is $25. On a larger $10,000 balance, 2.5% works out to $250. Your actual minimum payment depends on your card's specific rate and floor, so enter your own balance above for an exact figure.

Q: How much will my minimum credit card payment be?

A: Multiply your balance by your card’s minimum payment rate, then compare that against your card’s floor and take whichever is larger. At a common 2.5% rate, a $3,200 balance gives $80, a $5,000 balance gives $125, and a $10,000 balance gives $250. If the percentage works out below the floor — typically $25 to $40 depending on the issuer — you pay the floor instead. Enter your own balance, rate, and floor above for an exact figure.

Q: What is the average minimum payment on a credit card?

A: There is no single national figure, because the minimum depends on your balance and your card’s terms. In practice most US issuers use a percentage of the balance between 1% and 3%, combined with a fixed floor: Bank of America uses a $25 floor, Discover $35, and Chase $40. On a mid-sized balance the percentage almost always exceeds the floor, so the percentage is what you actually pay.

Q: How much of my minimum payment goes to interest?

A: Most of it. On a $3,200 balance at 21.9% APR, the first month’s interest is about $58.40 while the minimum payment is $80 — so roughly 73% of that payment covers interest and only about $21.60 reduces what you actually owe. That ratio stays close to 73% as the balance falls, because the required payment shrinks alongside the balance, which is why the debt takes so long to clear.

Q: Can you pay off a credit card by making only the minimum payment?

A: Yes, eventually, provided you stop adding new purchases — but it takes far longer than most people expect. A $3,200 balance at 21.9% APR clears in about 245 months, or roughly 20 years, and costs about $6,758 in interest. A $10,000 balance takes around 34 years. Minimum payments are designed to keep the account in good standing, not to clear the debt efficiently.

Q: What is the minimum payment on a $10,000 credit card balance?

A: At a 2.5% minimum payment rate, the first minimum payment on $10,000 is $250. Paying only that amount at 21.9% APR takes about 413 months — roughly 34 years — and costs about $25,143 in interest, which is more than twice the original balance. Doubling the payment cuts that dramatically. See the balance table above for other amounts.
