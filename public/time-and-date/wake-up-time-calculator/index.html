Wake-up times by bedtime

To find a wake-up time by hand, add the cycle length to the moment you actually fall asleep: 6 hours for 4 cycles, 7 hours 30 minutes for 5 cycles, and 9 hours for 6 cycles. The table below does that for common fall-asleep times so you can scan for one that fits your morning.

Worked example. Say you fall asleep at 11:00 pm. Four cycles is 6 hours, so the early alarm is 5:00 am. Five cycles is 7 hours 30 minutes, giving 6:30 am — the balanced default. Six cycles is 9 hours, or 8:00 am, when you can protect the time. If you are still getting into bed at 11:00 pm rather than already asleep, switch to bedtime mode: the calculator adds a 15-minute fall-asleep buffer, so five cycles then points to 6:45 am.

  • Waking at a cycle boundary is a trade-off against total sleep — a tidy 4-cycle alarm still leaves you short if the night was already too short.
  • Real cycles drift, so treat each row as the middle of a 20-30 minute window rather than an exact minute.

How many hours of sleep is that?

A common question is the reverse: given a bedtime and a wake time, how long is the night and how many cycles does it cover? At roughly 90 minutes per cycle, every 1.5 hours of sleep is one more cycle.

Why you wake up groggy even after enough sleep

Waking in the middle of deep sleep triggers sleep inertia — the heavy, slow-thinking feeling that can last anywhere from 15 to 60 minutes. Cycle-based wake times try to avoid it by landing you in lighter sleep, where waking feels easier.

The 90-minute figure is an average. A real sleep cycle runs closer to 70 to 120 minutes, it changes across the night, and it shifts with age, sleep debt, alcohol, and late caffeine. That is why a suggested wake-up time can still feel off on any given morning: the calculator is aiming at a moving target.

A few things reliably reduce morning grogginess: a consistent wake time seven days a week, bright light within a few minutes of getting up, and not stacking short nights back to back. If you wake mid-cycle anyway, a short delay before demanding tasks lets inertia clear.

How much sleep do you need by age

Cycle timing matters less if the total is wrong. Widely used public-health ranges for a full 24 hours are a helpful starting point, though individual needs vary by an hour or so either way.

  • Teenagers (14-17): about 8 to 10 hours, roughly 6 cycles.
  • Young adults and adults (18-64): about 7 to 9 hours, roughly 5 to 6 cycles.
  • Older adults (65+): about 7 to 8 hours, often with lighter, more broken sleep.

Match the cycle option to your age band first, then use the wake-up time to make the alarm land cleanly. Chasing a neat cycle boundary at the cost of an hour of total sleep is usually the wrong trade.

How to pick the best wake-up option

The strongest wake-up choice protects enough total sleep and still fits your fixed morning commitments. It is easy to focus only on the cleanest cycle boundary, but a technically tidy alarm can still leave you short on rest if it cuts the night too aggressively.

Many people use the 5-cycle option as the default planning target, keep 4 cycles as the early-start fallback, and use 6 cycles as the recovery option. That gives a realistic range for weekdays, travel mornings, and weekends without rebuilding the calculation each time your schedule changes.

  • 4 cycles: a shorter option for unavoidable early alarms.
  • 5 cycles: a balanced target for many standard work or school mornings.
  • 6 cycles: a longer option when recovery and next-day alertness matter more.

FAQ

How many sleep cycles should I aim for?

Most adults feel best after 5 or 6 complete cycles, which is roughly 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep. Four cycles, about 6 hours, works for an occasional early start, but a run of short nights builds sleep debt. Your own ideal number can sit slightly outside that range.

What is a sleep cycle?

A sleep cycle is one full pass through light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep. It averages about 90 minutes but realistically ranges from 70 to 120 minutes, and the early cycles of the night hold more deep sleep while later ones hold more REM.

What time should I wake up if I go to sleep now?

Add 6 hours for 4 cycles, 7 hours 30 minutes for 5 cycles, or 9 hours for 6 cycles to the time you actually fall asleep. If you are getting into bed now rather than already drifting off, add another 10 to 20 minutes first. The "I'm going to sleep now" button does this from the current time.

If I sleep at 10 and wake up at 6, how many hours is that?

That is 8 hours, or about five and a third 90-minute cycles. Sleeping from 10 pm to 7 am is 9 hours, which is 6 full cycles, and 10 pm to 5:30 am is 7.5 hours, which is 5 cycles. The sleep-duration table above lists more common pairs.

Is it better to wake up after 5 or 6 sleep cycles?

Six cycles gives more total sleep and more REM, so it is the better choice when you can protect the time. Five cycles is the common weekday compromise because it still clears 7 hours while fitting a normal schedule. Pick the option you can repeat consistently rather than the longest single night.

Why do I still feel groggy after a suggested wake-up time?

Cycle length varies from night to night, so an alarm set to an average can still land in deep sleep. Sleep debt, alcohol, late caffeine, and waking in darkness all deepen that grogginess, which is called sleep inertia. It usually fades within 15 to 60 minutes.

Does the calculator assume it takes time to fall asleep?

In bedtime mode it adds a fixed 15-minute fall-asleep buffer before the first cycle starts. In fall-asleep mode it assumes you are already asleep at the time you enter, so no buffer is added. Most healthy sleepers take 10 to 20 minutes to drop off.

Can I use this if I wake up during the night?

Yes. Enter the time you expect to fall back asleep and calculate again for updated wake-up options. A long awakening effectively restarts the cycle count for the rest of the night.

What if my schedule only allows 4 cycles?

Four cycles, roughly 6 hours, can carry you through an occasional early start, especially if you wake at a cycle boundary rather than mid-cycle. Repeated 4-cycle nights build a sleep debt that shows up as slower reactions and worse mood. Treat it as a fallback, not a plan.

Does daylight saving time change the result?

The cycle maths is the same, but the clock jump shifts your wall-clock wake time by an hour and your body clock takes a few days to catch up. Around the changeover, lean toward the longer 6-cycle option.

How is a wake-up time calculator different from an alarm?

An alarm rings at whatever time you set. This tool works back from sleep cycles to suggest times that fall between them, so the alarm is less likely to interrupt deep sleep and trigger sleep inertia.

Is this a medical tool?

No. It is a scheduling aid built on population averages and does not diagnose anything. Persistent trouble waking, heavy daytime sleepiness, or unrefreshing sleep is worth raising with a doctor.

Does it store the times I enter?

No. Every calculation runs locally in your browser, and nothing is sent to a server or saved.
