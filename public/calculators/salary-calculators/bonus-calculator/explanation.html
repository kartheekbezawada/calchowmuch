Incentive pay guide

How a bonus calculator turns a percentage or payout into total compensation

This bonus calculator takes a base salary and either a bonus percentage or a flat bonus amount, and returns the bonus in dollars, your total gross compensation for the period, and the effective bonus percentage. It answers the practical question behind a job offer, a performance review, or a compensation letter: what is this bonus actually worth on top of my salary, and how big is it next to my base pay? Because bonus pay is variable and is often quoted as a “target” rather than a promise, seeing the dollar figure and the percentage together makes an incentive package easier to judge than a headline percentage on its own.

Every figure here is gross — before income tax, payroll tax, and any bonus-specific withholding. That keeps the number comparable across offers, which is usually what you want when you weigh one package against another.

How to Guide

What this calculator does

It converts a bonus assumption into three figures: the bonus amount, total compensation (salary plus bonus), and the bonus as a percentage of salary. Percent mode is for bonuses tied to base pay, such as a “15% target bonus”. Flat-amount mode is for a fixed payout you have already been quoted, such as a $10,000 signing bonus. Switching modes lets you check the same package from either direction.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your base salary.
  2. Pick percent mode (bonus tied to salary) or flat-amount mode (bonus already known).
  3. Enter the bonus percentage or the flat bonus amount.
  4. Select Calculate to see the bonus amount, total compensation, and effective bonus percentage.
  5. Compare the total against another offer, or against last year’s package, to see the real change.

How to interpret the results

Result What it tells you
Bonus amount The gross value of the bonus by itself, before any tax or withholding.
Total compensation Base salary plus the bonus — the number to compare between two offers.
Bonus percent How large the bonus is relative to base pay, so a flat payout and a percentage target can be compared on the same scale.

Read total compensation first when you compare two packages, then use the bonus percentage to see how much of each one is guaranteed base pay versus pay that depends on performance, company results, or staying through a vesting date.

Worked example

Two ways to look at a $60,000 salary. In percent mode with a 15% target bonus, the bonus amount is $9,000 and total compensation is $69,000. In flat-amount mode with an $8,000 payout, the effective bonus works out to 13.33% and total compensation is $68,000. The percentage view makes it clear that the $9,000 target is the slightly richer package, even though an $8,000 cheque sounds close.

A bonus is not a raise

This is the distinction that trips people up. A 10% raise on a $60,000 salary adds $6,000 to your base every year, and next year’s raise is calculated on the higher figure, so it compounds. A 10% bonus adds $6,000 once; if it is not repeated, your base pay is unchanged and there is nothing to compound. Over five years a modest recurring raise usually beats a one-off bonus of the same headline size, sometimes by a wide margin.

The trade-off is stability. A higher base is money you can budget around; a larger bonus is upside that may or may not arrive. When an offer leans on bonus rather than base, treat the bonus as the variable part of the deal — ask whether it is discretionary or formula-based, what it has paid out in recent years, and whether it is prorated if you join or leave mid-year.

When a bonus flatters an offer

A large target bonus can make a total-compensation number look competitive while the guaranteed part of the offer sits below market. Use the bonus percentage to see how exposed the package is: a 5% bonus is a rounding error on your budget, while a 40% bonus means nearly a third of your quoted pay is contingent.

For anything you rely on every month — rent, a mortgage, loan payments — plan around base salary and treat the bonus as a chance to reduce debt or add to savings when it lands, rather than as regular income.

Edge cases and limits

  • Zero bonus: enter 0 in either mode to drop total compensation back to base salary — useful for modelling a year where the bonus does not pay out. The minimum is 0 and there is no maximum, so you can also stress-test an unusually large payout.
  • Bonus larger than salary: allowed, and common for founders, high-upside sales roles, or equity-heavy packages; the effective percentage simply goes above 100%.
  • Multiple bonuses: the calculator models one bonus input at a time. For a signing bonus plus an annual bonus, add them into a single flat amount or run the tool twice.
  • Reverse solving: enter salary and a known bonus in flat-amount mode and the effective percentage is calculated for you — handy for checking your offer against a published target-bonus range.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is a bonus calculated from salary?

In percent mode, bonus amount equals salary multiplied by the bonus percentage. In flat-amount mode you enter the bonus directly and the tool works out what percentage of salary it represents.

What is total compensation here?

Base salary plus the bonus amount, before tax. It is the figure to compare when two offers split pay differently between base and bonus.

Is a bonus taxed more than salary?

Not in the end. US employers often withhold a flat 22% on supplemental pay such as bonuses, and other countries use a similar shortcut, but your actual tax is settled on total income when you file. The higher withholding is a cash-flow timing effect, not a higher tax rate.

Does this calculator apply any tax?

No. It returns gross bonus and gross total compensation. For take-home after tax, run the total through the Salary Calculator.

Can I reverse a known bonus into a percentage?

Yes. Enter your salary and the bonus in flat-amount mode; the effective bonus percentage is shown next to total compensation.

Should I count a target bonus as guaranteed income?

Usually not. A target is what pays out if performance and company results land as expected. Check the recent payout history and whether it is discretionary before you budget around it.

How does a bonus compare with a raise of the same size?

A raise changes base pay permanently and compounds into future raises; a one-off bonus does neither. Model both in the Raise Calculator to see the multi-year gap.

What about a signing bonus that is clawed back if I leave early?

Model it as flat-amount pay for the first year only, and read the clawback terms carefully: many require repayment of the gross amount even though you received a smaller net payment.

Can the bonus be more than 100% of salary?

Yes. The calculator accepts any non-negative amount, so equity-heavy or commission-style packages where variable pay exceeds base are handled — the effective percentage just goes above 100%.
