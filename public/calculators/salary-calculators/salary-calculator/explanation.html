Salary and pay conversion guide

Convert your salary between every pay period — then add tax if you need it

Most of the time the question is simple arithmetic: your salary is quoted one way and you need it another way. An annual salary as a monthly figure. An hourly rate as a yearly salary. A biweekly paycheck as a monthly amount. This calculator does that conversion first, with no tax and no country selected: enter one figure at its pay frequency and read it back as annual, monthly, 4-weekly, biweekly, weekly, daily and hourly.

When you also want the number that actually reaches your bank account, add a country. Pick UK for take-home after Income Tax, National Insurance, pension and student loan; United States for federal income tax, FICA and your state's own income and payroll taxes; or Canada for federal and provincial income tax, CPP and EI. Adding a country layers a full deductions breakdown, effective and marginal rates, and a pay-date sheet on top of the conversion — and you can switch it back off at any time.

How to Guide

Step 1. Enter your pay and its frequency

Enter the amount you know, then select the frequency it refers to. This matters more than people expect: 3,000 with "Monthly" selected produces a very different result from 3,000 with "Weekly" selected. The calculator normalises whatever you enter into an annual figure internally, then works back out to every other period — so it converts annual to monthly, hourly to salary, biweekly to monthly, weekly to annual or any other pairing from the one figure you type.

By default no country is selected, so the result is a plain pay conversion with no tax applied — the currency symbol is dropped because the arithmetic is identical everywhere. Read the answer in the per-period cards and the summary panel.

Step 2. Convert from an hourly or daily rate

If you are paid hourly or daily, open Work schedule assumptions and set your real hours per week, weeks per year and workdays per week so the annualised figure matches your contract rather than a generic full-time pattern. Hourly and daily are the only conversions that depend on your schedule, because there is no fixed number of working hours in a year.

Step 3. Add a country for take-home (optional)

If you want the after-tax figure, add UK, United States or Canada. Clicking an active country again removes it and returns you to the plain conversion.

With UK selected, choose England, Wales, Northern Ireland or Scotland. This is not cosmetic. Scotland operates its own income tax bands with additional rates that the rest of the UK does not use, so the same salary produces a different take-home figure depending on where you are resident for tax purposes.

With United States selected, type your state into the search box and pick it from the list, then choose your filing status. There is deliberately no default state: nine states levy no income tax on wages while others reach into double digits, so assuming one would silently produce a number that is wrong for most people.

With Canada selected, type your province or territory into the search box and pick it from the list - it defaults to Alberta, but every one of the 13 provinces and territories changes the result. Quebec changes the most: it swaps CPP for QPP, adds a separate QPIP premium, reduces the EI rate, and applies a federal tax abatement on top of its own provincial bands.

Step 4. Add only what applies to you

Everything beyond gross salary and region is hidden until you ask for it. The optional controls are grouped into three headings. Tap a chip to reveal just that section:

Deductions to add — amounts taken out of your pay:

  • Pension (UK): your contribution percentage and the relief method your scheme uses.
  • 401(k) / pre-tax (USA): annual pre-tax contributions. These cut your income tax but not your FICA.
  • Student loan (UK): your plan, plus a separate box if you also hold a Postgraduate Loan.

Optional extra payments — amounts added on top of your base salary. They do not change your base pay until you add them, and each one is annualised and folded into your gross for tax:

  • Overtime: enter it as a fixed amount, as an hourly rate times hours worked, or as a percentage of a gross-pay basis (hourly, weekly, 4-weekly, monthly or annual).
  • Bonus: the amount, plus how often it is paid (one-off, or recurring at a set frequency).
  • Commission: the amount and its frequency, converted to an annual figure before it is added.
  • Other: any further taxable payment - shift allowance, on-call pay, a stipend - with its own frequency.

Pay schedule: your first pay date and how weekend paydays are handled. Optional - leave it alone and the pay sheet assumes the 1st of each month.

Step 5. Read your results

With no country selected, the large figure is your annual pay and the cards below break it into monthly, 4-weekly (annual ÷ 13), biweekly, weekly, daily and hourly. Add a country and the large figure becomes your estimated take-home pay, the bar beneath it shows where your gross salary went - the green segment is what you keep, the rest is tax, National Insurance or FICA - and the Pay dates sheet lower down shows the actual days you will be paid.

With a country selected the Deductions section lists every statutory and voluntary deduction as its own line, in the terms that country uses: Income Tax, National Insurance, pension and student loan for the UK; federal tax, state tax, Social Security, Medicare and pre-tax contributions for the US; federal tax, provincial tax, CPP or QPP, EI and any health premium for Canada. The lines add up to the same annual total shown in the bar.

Step 6. Expand the calculation

"See how this was calculated" opens a band-by-band breakdown: your allowance or standard deduction, how much income fell into each tax band, and every deduction listed individually. If a number looks wrong, open this first. It usually shows which assumption differs from your situation.

What this result means

The headline figure is what you keep across a full year. To interpret it properly, read it alongside the two rates shown beside it. The effective rate tells you what proportion of your gross pay disappears in total; the marginal rate tells you what a pay rise would actually be worth. A scenario where those two numbers are far apart usually means you are near a threshold, and the breakdown will show which one.

Use it to compare like with like. Running each job offer as its own scenario, with its own pension percentage and hours, is the only reliable way to see which one leaves you better off. A higher gross salary with a worse pension can be the weaker deal once the trade-off is visible.

How to convert a salary between pay periods

With no country selected the calculator applies no tax at all. It answers one question precisely: if you know your pay as one figure, what is the same pay expressed every other way? That covers annual to monthly, hourly to salary, biweekly to monthly, weekly to annual, yearly to weekly and every other pairing, from the one amount you enter.

The conversion factors

Whatever you enter is normalised into an annual figure first, then divided back out. Every period is a fixed divisor of the annual salary, except daily and hourly, which depend on your work schedule:

Pay period From annual salary Example: £52,000 a year
Monthlyannual ÷ 12£4,333.33
4-weeklyannual ÷ 13£4,000.00
Biweekly (fortnightly)annual ÷ 26£2,000.00

Dailyweekly ÷ workdays per week£200.00 (5-day week)

To go the other way, multiply: an hourly rate becomes an annual salary as rate × hours per week × weeks per year, a weekly wage becomes annual as × 52, and a biweekly paycheck becomes annual as × 26.

Worked examples

  • Annual to monthly. £30,000 a year ÷ 12 = £2,500 a month. $25,975 a year ÷ 12 = about $2,164.58 a month. If you earn 25k a year, that is roughly 2,083 a month; 60k a year is 5,000 a month.
  • Annual to weekly. £43,700 a year ÷ 52 = £840.38 a week. $65,000 a year ÷ 52 = $1,250 a week. The "60000 divided by 52 weeks" shortcut gives £1,153.85.
  • Biweekly to annual and to monthly. $2,769.23 paid every two weeks × 26 = about $72,000 a year, which is $6,000 a month. Note biweekly ≠ twice monthly: 26 paychecks a year, not 24.
  • Hourly to salary. $32.69 an hour × 40 hours × 52 weeks = about $67,995 a year, or roughly $2,615 biweekly. $50 an hour full-time is about $8,667 a month.
  • Daily rate from an annual salary. £52,000 a year ÷ 52 weeks ÷ 5 workdays = £200 a day. Change the workdays-per-week figure for a part-time or compressed week.

Why the figures have no currency symbol

The conversion view drops the currency symbol on purpose: the arithmetic is identical in any currency, and the tool is used well outside the UK, US and Canada. Add a country and the symbol appears, because at that point the calculation genuinely is country-specific.

UK deductions explained

Income Tax

UK Income Tax is progressive: different portions of your income are taxed at different rates. Everyone starts with a Personal Allowance taxed at 0%. Income above that is taxed at the basic rate, then the higher rate, then the additional rate as you cross each threshold. Only the portion above a threshold is taxed at the higher rate. Entering a new band does not re-tax everything you earn.

The £100,000 Personal Allowance taper

Once your income exceeds £100,000, the Personal Allowance is reduced by £1 for every £2 above that threshold, reaching zero at £125,140. Because you lose allowance at the same time you pay higher-rate tax on the income causing the loss, this creates an effective marginal rate of about 60% across that band. Often more than the additional rate above it. The calculator models the taper rather than approximating it, and flags when you are inside it.

This is the edge case where a bonus behaves most unexpectedly. A lump sum paid to someone already earning near £100,000 can be taxed at an effective 62% once the lost allowance and National Insurance are counted, which is why the bonus figure is calculated as the difference between two full results rather than by applying a single rate.

National Insurance

National Insurance is a separate deduction with its own thresholds, calculated on gross earnings rather than on income after your Personal Allowance. Employee contributions apply at one rate between the Primary Threshold and the Upper Earnings Limit, and at a much lower rate above the upper limit. Any calculator that applies a single flat percentage to your gross pay will be wrong.

Pension contributions and why the relief method matters

A pension contribution is not simply a percentage off your gross pay. The relief method changes the arithmetic, and getting it wrong is the most common source of incorrect UK take-home figures.

  • Net pay arrangement: the contribution comes out of gross pay before Income Tax, so it reduces your taxable income. National Insurance is still charged on the full amount.
  • Salary sacrifice: the contribution reduces gross pay before both Income Tax and National Insurance, so it also cuts your NI bill. This is why sacrifice is worth more than a net pay arrangement at the same percentage.
  • Relief at source: the contribution comes out of net pay and the scheme reclaims basic-rate relief on your behalf, so your take-home falls by only 80% of the nominal amount. Higher-rate relief is claimed separately through Self Assessment and does not appear in your take-home pay.

Two limits are worth knowing. Auto-enrolment sets a minimum total contribution, normally split between you and your employer, so most workplace schemes start there rather than at zero. At the other end, the annual allowance caps the maximum you can contribute with tax relief in a year, and it tapers down for very high earners. This calculator applies the percentage you enter without enforcing either limit, so check your scheme's rules if you are close to one.

Student loans

Student loan repayments are a percentage of income above a plan-specific threshold, calculated entirely separately from Income Tax and National Insurance. Plans 1, 2, 4, 5 and Postgraduate each have their own threshold and rate. If you hold both an undergraduate plan and a postgraduate loan, both are deducted at the same time. That is why the plan selector and the postgraduate checkbox are separate controls.

US deductions explained

Federal income tax

Federal income tax is progressive: different portions of your income are taxed at different rates, from 10% up to 37%. Only the portion above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate - moving into a new bracket does not re-tax everything you earn. Your standard deduction is subtracted first, so tax is charged on what remains rather than on your full salary.

Filing status changes more than people expect

The brackets and the standard deduction both vary by filing status. For 2026 the standard deduction is $16,100 filing Single or Married Filing Separately, $24,150 as Head of Household, and $32,200 Married Filing Jointly. On a $100,000 salary that difference alone is worth thousands: filing Single produces about $13,170 of federal tax, while Married Filing Jointly produces about $7,640 on the same income.

One detail worth knowing if you file separately: Married Filing Separately shares the Single brackets right up until the top rate, where it switches at $384,350 rather than $640,600 - exactly half the Married Filing Jointly threshold.

FICA - Social Security and Medicare

FICA is separate from income tax and is charged on your gross wages, not on income after the standard deduction. Social Security is 6.2% up to an annual wage base of $184,500 for 2026, above which no further Social Security tax is due. Medicare is 1.45% with no cap at all, and an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% is withheld on wages above $200,000.

Because FICA is charged on gross pay, a 401(k) contribution reduces your income tax but not your FICA. This is the detail most paycheck calculators get wrong, and it means the true saving from a pre-tax contribution is smaller than the headline rate suggests.

State income tax

This is where US take-home diverges most sharply. Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming levy no income tax on wages at all. Around fifteen states apply a single flat rate. The rest use graduated brackets with their own standard deductions and personal exemptions, which frequently differ from the federal rules.

The effect is large enough to change which job offer is better. The same $100,000 salary leaves roughly $79,180 in Texas but about $72,672 in California. A gap of over $6,500 a year from state taxes alone.

State payroll taxes

Beyond income tax, some states levy employee-side payroll contributions that come straight off your paycheck: State Disability Insurance, Paid Family and Medical Leave, and in a few states an employee unemployment contribution. California SDI is 1.3% of all wages with no cap. New Jersey charges TDI and FLI separately, and New York deducts Paid Family Leave. These are easy to overlook but represent real money every payday.

Local and city income taxes

A number of US cities and counties levy their own income tax on top of federal and state tax. New York City and Yonkers, Philadelphia, most Ohio municipalities, several Maryland counties, Detroit, St. Louis, Kansas City, Louisville and Birmingham all apply local rates to residents, non-resident workers, or both.

This calculator does not include local income tax. If you live or work in one of the jurisdictions above, your actual take-home will be lower than the figure shown - for a New York City resident the difference can run to several thousand dollars a year. Treat the USA estimate as federal, FICA and state-level only.

Canada deductions explained

Federal income tax and the Basic Personal Amount

Federal tax is progressive, from 14% up to 33% across five bands. Every taxpayer's full income is charged band by band first - unlike the UK or US, Canada does not remove a tax-free amount from income before the bands apply. Instead, the Basic Personal Amount (BPA) works as a non-refundable tax credit: the BPA figure is multiplied by the lowest band's rate, and that credit is subtracted from the tax bill afterwards. For 2026 the federal BPA is $16,452, worth a credit of about $2,303.

The BPA taper for higher earners

Above $181,440 of net income, the federal BPA starts shrinking, reaching a floor of $14,829 by $258,482. Unlike the UK Personal Allowance, which tapers all the way to zero, Canada's federal BPA never disappears completely - it just settles at a lower, fixed amount for very high earners.

CPP and the newer CPP2 tier

The Canada Pension Plan charges 5.95% on earnings between a $3,500 exemption and the year's maximum pensionable earnings ($74,600 for 2026). A second tier, CPP2, was introduced alongside the base rate and charges an additional 4.0% on earnings between that ceiling and a higher one ($85,000 for 2026). It is a genuinely separate contribution, not a continuation of the base CPP band, so someone earning $80,000 pays CPP2 on the $5,400 above the first ceiling.

Employment Insurance (EI)

EI is a flat 1.66% of gross earnings up to a Maximum Insurable Earnings ceiling ($68,900 for 2026), with nothing further owed above it - the same capped shape as US Social Security.

Provincial or territorial tax works the same way, with its own numbers

Every province and territory layers its own progressive bands and its own Basic Personal Amount credit on top of federal tax, calculated the same credit-based way. Ontario adds a surtax - an extra percentage charged on provincial tax itself once it crosses two thresholds - plus a separate, income-tested health premium worth up to $900 a year. Neither exists in any other province in this calculator.

Quebec runs its own system entirely

Quebec is the one province that changes more than its bracket table. Quebec residents contribute to the Quebec Pension Plan (QPP) instead of CPP, at a combined base-plus-first-additional rate of 6.3% (plus a matching 4.0% QPP2 tier). They pay a reduced Employment Insurance rate - 1.30% instead of 1.66% - because a separate Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP) premium covers what EI's parental and maternity benefits would otherwise fund federally. And because Quebec collects its own provincial tax directly through Revenu Québec rather than through the usual federal arrangement, residents receive a federal abatement that reduces federal tax owing by 16.5%.

Pay frequency, extra payments and your pay sheet

Pay frequency explained

Pay frequency changes the size of each payslip. It does not change your annual total. The distinction that causes the most confusion is between biweekly and four-weekly pay, which sound alike but produce a different number of payslips.

Frequency Payslips per year Each payslip
Weekly52Annual pay divided by 52
Biweekly26Annual pay divided by 26, paid every 14 days
Four-weekly13Annual pay divided by 13, paid every 28 days
Monthly12Annual pay divided by 12

Because a year runs slightly longer than 52 weeks, biweekly employees receive three payslips in two months of most years. That is worth budgeting around rather than treating as extra income.

Four-weekly pay is always calculated as annual pay divided by 13. Deriving it from a rounded monthly figure introduces small errors that never quite reconcile against a real payslip.

How overtime, bonuses and commission are handled

Overtime, a bonus, commission and any "other" payment are all taxable income on top of your base salary. Each one is entered with its own frequency - one-off, weekly, 4-weekly, monthly or annual - and converted to an annual amount before anything else happens. A £400 monthly commission becomes £4,800 a year; a one-off £2,000 bonus stays £2,000.

Overtime can be entered three ways: a fixed amount, an hourly rate multiplied by the hours worked, or a percentage applied to a gross-pay basis you choose (your hourly, weekly, 4-weekly, monthly or annual gross). All three resolve to a single annual figure.

The calculator then adds the combined annual extra to your salary, calculates total tax on that higher figure, and shows the take-home that results. Working the tax on the combined total is more accurate than applying a single marginal rate, because the extra can push part of your income into a higher band or reduce an allowance you would otherwise have kept. On the pay sheet the extra is spread evenly across your paydays rather than shown as one lump, so every row reconciles to the same annual total.

Using the pay dates sheet

The sheet lists your next twelve paydays with the gross amount, each deduction, and the net figure landing in your account. It appears once you add a country and needs no setup.

Setting a first pay date is optional. Leave it blank and the sheet assumes the first of each month. Enter your real date and every row updates to match your payroll.

One behaviour is worth knowing. Where a payday falls on a Saturday or Sunday, most employers pay on the preceding Friday, and shifted rows are marked so you can see the rule applied. Any overtime, bonus or commission you have added is spread evenly across the paydays, so each row reflects your average pay rather than trying to guess which payslip a lump sum lands on.

Worked example. $100,000 in California versus Texas

Start with the parts that are identical in both states. Filing Single, the standard deduction of $16,100 leaves $83,900 of taxable income. That produces $1,240 at 10%, $4,560 at 12% and $7,370 at 22% - a federal tax charge of $13,170. FICA adds $6,200 of Social Security and $1,450 of Medicare, so $7,650 more. Neither figure depends on where you live.

In Texas that is the whole story: no state income tax and no employee-side payroll contribution, leaving an estimated $79,180 a year, or about $6,598 a month. The effective rate is 20.8%.

In California, a state standard deduction of $5,706 applies before ten graduated bands running from 1% to 13.3%, producing roughly $5,208 of state income tax. CA SDI adds a further 1.3% of all wages, or $1,300. Take-home falls to about $72,672 a year. An effective rate of 27.3%, and over $6,500 a year less than the identical salary in Texas.

Worked example. £60,000 in England

A Personal Allowance of £12,570 is taxed at 0%. The next £37,700 falls in the basic-rate band at 20%, producing £7,540. The remaining £9,730 falls into the higher-rate band at 40%, producing £3,892. A total Income Tax charge of £11,432. National Insurance adds approximately £3,211, calculated on gross earnings with its own thresholds rather than on income after the allowance.

With no pension or student loan, that leaves an estimated £60,000 take-home of about £45,357 a year — roughly £3,780 a month, £3,489 per 4-weekly, £872 a week.

Worked example. $60,000 in Alberta versus Quebec

Start with federal tax, which is identical in both. $60,000 charged band by band produces $8,496 of tax before credits. The federal Basic Personal Amount of $16,452 is worth a credit of $2,303 at the 14% rate, bringing federal tax owing down to $6,193.

In Alberta, CPP takes 5.95% of the $56,500 above the exemption - $3,362 - and EI takes 1.66% of the full salary, $996. Alberta's own provincial tax follows the same band-then-credit pattern as federal, landing at $2,978 after its own Basic Personal Amount credit. No surtax, no health premium. That leaves an estimated take-home of about $46,471 a year, or roughly $3,873 a month.

In Quebec, the whole deduction stack changes shape. QPP replaces CPP at a higher combined rate, taking $3,560. EI drops to the Quebec-reduced rate, $780, but a separate QPIP premium adds $258. Quebec's own provincial tax, with its own bands and Basic Personal Amount, comes to $6,029 - well above Alberta's provincial figure. Working the other way, the federal abatement gives back $1,022 of the federal tax charged above. Net result: an estimated take-home of about $44,202 a year, roughly $2,269 less than the identical salary in Alberta.

Common mistakes when comparing salaries

  • Comparing gross against net. A lower headline salary can be the better offer once a stronger pension contribution is counted. Compare like with like.
  • Ignoring the schedule. A salary quoted for 37.5 hours a week is not equivalent to the same figure for 45 hours. Convert to hourly before deciding.
  • Confusing effective and marginal rates. Entering a higher tax band does not mean all your income is taxed at that rate. Only the portion above the threshold is.
  • Counting on a bonus before you receive it. Discretionary bonuses are not contractual salary and may not repeat. Budget on your base pay.
  • Assuming pension percentage equals cost. A 5% contribution reduces take-home by less than 5% of gross, because part of it would otherwise have gone to tax.

What to do with what's left

Take-home pay is only the starting point. Once you know the number that actually reaches your account, the next question is what it has to cover. And high-interest debt is usually the item that quietly consumes the most of it.

  • If you carry a credit card balance, see what minimum-only payments really cost. On a typical card, paying only the minimum can take close to 26 years and more than double the total cost.
  • Compare that against a fixed monthly repayment with the credit card payoff calculator to see how much time and interest a higher payment saves.
  • If you are juggling several balances, the debt repayment calculator works out which debt to pay off first and when you would be debt-free.

FAQ

Does this calculator show take-home pay or gross pay?

Both. By default, with no country selected, it converts one gross amount into every pay period with no tax applied. Add UK, US or Canada and it also estimates take-home pay after Income Tax, National Insurance or FICA, pension and student loan.

How do I convert an annual salary to monthly pay?

Divide the annual salary by 12. Enter your salary with "Annual" selected and the monthly figure is shown in the results, along with 4-weekly (annual divided by 13), weekly and every other period. For example, £30,000 a year is £2,500 a month, and $25,975 a year is about $2,164.58 a month.

How do I convert biweekly pay to a monthly or annual figure?

Multiply the biweekly amount by 26 for the annual salary, then divide by 12 for the monthly figure. Biweekly is not the same as twice monthly, because there are 26 fortnightly paychecks in a year, not 24. Enter the amount with "Biweekly" selected and the calculator does both steps.

How do I convert an hourly rate to an annual salary?

Multiply the hourly rate by your hours per week and then by your weeks per year. At 40 hours and 52 weeks, $32.69 an hour is about $67,995 a year. Set your real hours per week, weeks per year and workdays per week under Work schedule assumptions so the annual figure matches your contract.

How is UK take-home pay calculated here?

Your Personal Allowance is applied first, then Income Tax is charged band by band on what remains. National Insurance is calculated separately on gross earnings using its own thresholds, and student loan repayments are a percentage of income above your plan threshold.

Why does Scotland give a different result?

Scotland sets its own income tax bands and rates, which differ from England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The same salary produces a different take-home figure depending on where you are resident for tax purposes, which is why the region selector is not optional.

Does a pension contribution reduce my tax?

It depends on the relief method. A net pay arrangement reduces taxable income but not National Insurance. Salary sacrifice reduces both, so it is worth more at the same percentage. Relief at source reduces neither, and the scheme reclaims basic-rate relief on your behalf.

What is the difference between the effective rate and the marginal rate?

The effective rate is your total deductions as a percentage of gross pay. The marginal rate is the rate charged on your next pound earned. The marginal rate is almost always higher, and confusing the two is the most common misunderstanding about how tax works.

How are overtime, bonuses and commission taxed?

Each extra payment is converted to an annual amount using the frequency you set, added to your salary, and the total tax is worked out on the combined figure. That is more accurate than applying your marginal rate to the extra, because it can push part of your income into a higher band.

How do I enter overtime?

Choose one of three methods: a fixed amount, an hourly rate multiplied by hours worked, or a percentage of a gross-pay basis you pick - hourly, weekly, 4-weekly, monthly or annual. Set how often that overtime occurs and the calculator annualises it before adding it to your gross.

Why is 4-weekly pay not the same as monthly pay?

Four-weekly pay means every 28 days, which is 13 payments a year. Monthly pay is 12 payments a year. The calculator divides annual pay by 13 for 4-weekly, never by taking a monthly figure and adjusting it.

Can I see the dates I will actually be paid?

Yes. Turn on Pay schedule and set your first pay date. The pay sheet lists your next 12 paydays with the gross, each deduction and the net amount landing on each one. Any extra payments are spread evenly across those paydays.

Why does my payslip differ from this estimate?

Payroll systems apply withholding rules that can differ from your annual tax position, and your employer may apply benefits, salary sacrifice arrangements or deductions not modelled here. This calculator estimates your annual position rather than replicating one payroll run.

Are my salary details stored anywhere?

No. All calculations run locally in your browser - no data is stored.

How is US take-home pay calculated here?

Your standard deduction is applied first, then federal income tax is charged bracket by bracket on what remains. FICA is calculated separately on your gross wages, and state income tax uses that state's own schedule and deductions.

Does my 401(k) reduce FICA as well as income tax?

No. A 401(k) reduces federal and state taxable income, but Social Security and Medicare are charged on your full gross wages regardless. This is the detail most paycheck calculators get wrong, and it makes the real saving smaller than the headline rate suggests.

Why does my state change the result so much?

Nine states levy no income tax on wages at all, around fifteen apply a single flat rate, and the rest use graduated brackets with their own deductions. On a $100,000 salary the difference between Texas and California is over $6,500 a year.

Are city or local income taxes included?

No. This calculator covers federal, FICA and state-level taxes only. If you live or work in a city with its own income tax. New York City, Philadelphia, Detroit and others. Your actual take-home will be lower than shown.

Which filing status should I choose?

Pick the status you file under: Single, Married Filing Jointly, Married Filing Separately, or Head of Household. Both the tax brackets and the standard deduction change with it, so on a $100,000 salary the federal tax alone can differ by several thousand dollars.

How is Canada take-home pay calculated here?

Federal tax is charged band by band on your full income, then a Basic Personal Amount credit is subtracted from the tax bill - not from the income being taxed, which is a different mechanic than the UK or US use. Provincial or territorial tax works the same way with its own bands and its own Basic Personal Amount credit. CPP and EI are calculated separately on gross earnings.

Why does my province change the result so much?

Each province and territory sets its own income tax bands and its own Basic Personal Amount. Ontario also adds a surtax and a health premium on top of provincial tax, and Quebec runs an entirely separate system - so the same salary can produce a noticeably different take-home figure depending on where you work.

What is CPP2, and why is it separate from CPP?

CPP2 is a second, additional contribution introduced alongside the base Canada Pension Plan rate. It applies only to earnings between the base ceiling (the YMPE) and a higher ceiling (the YAMPE), at its own rate - it is not simply a continuation of the base CPP band.

Is Quebec calculated differently from other provinces?

Yes. Quebec residents contribute to the Quebec Pension Plan (QPP) instead of CPP, pay a reduced Employment Insurance rate alongside a separate Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP) premium, and receive a federal tax abatement that reduces federal tax owing to reflect Quebec collecting its own provincial tax directly.

Does the Basic Personal Amount work like the UK Personal Allowance?

Not quite. The UK Personal Allowance reduces the income that gets taxed. Canada's Basic Personal Amount instead reduces the tax bill directly, as a credit equal to the amount multiplied by the lowest tax rate - full tax is charged on the lowest band first, then the credit is subtracted from what is owed.
