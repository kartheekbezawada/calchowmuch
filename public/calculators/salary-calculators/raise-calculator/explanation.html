Pay increase guide

How a raise calculator shows the size of a pay increase, and whether it is real

This raise calculator takes your current salary and a raise expressed as a percentage or a flat amount, and returns the raise amount, the new salary, and the percent increase. Those three numbers turn a vague “we can offer you a bit more” into something you can judge. The calculator also helps with the question people forget to ask at review time: once inflation is taken into account, is the increase actually making you better off, or just keeping you level?

Every figure is gross — before income tax and deductions. A raise is permanent in a way a bonus is not: it changes your base pay, and next year’s raise is calculated on the higher figure, so increases compound over a career.

How to Guide

What this calculator does

It converts a raise into three figures: the raise amount in dollars, your new salary, and the raise as a percentage of your current pay. Percent mode multiplies current salary by the percentage. Flat-amount mode uses the number you enter and works out the equivalent percentage, so you can compare two offers even when they are quoted differently.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your current salary.
  2. Choose percent mode (raise tied to salary) or flat-amount mode (raise already quoted).
  3. Enter the raise percentage or the flat raise amount.
  4. Select Calculate to see the raise amount, new salary, and percent increase.
  5. Check the new salary against inflation and your market using the sections below.

How to interpret the results

Result What it tells you
Raise amount The direct dollar increase to gross pay — the figure that hits your budget.
New salary Your updated gross annual pay, and the base every future raise builds on.
Percent increase The raise relative to current pay, so a flat amount and a percentage offer can be compared on the same scale.

Use the raise amount to judge the immediate impact and the percent increase to compare it against inflation, your sector’s typical raise, and your last increase.

Worked example

On a $60,000 salary, a 5% raise is a raise amount of $3,000 and a new salary of $63,000. A flat $4,000 raise instead gives a new salary of $64,000 and an equivalent increase of 6.67%. Seeing both lets you tell a “5%” and a “$4,000” offer apart at a glance.

Percentage or dollars — why both matter

A percentage grows with your salary, so the same 5% is worth very different money to two people. A flat raise does the opposite: its effective percentage depends entirely on where you start. The dollar figure tells you what changes in your monthly budget; the percentage tells you how the offer compares with everything else — inflation, market data, and what colleagues are getting.

Is the raise real? Checking it against inflation

A raise only improves your life if it grows faster than prices. To check, multiply your old salary by (1 + inflation rate) for each year since your last raise. That is the salary you need just to stand still. If your new salary is above it, the raise is a real gain; if it is below, the “raise” is a real pay cut.

Example: your salary goes from $60,000 to $63,000 over one year in which inflation averaged 4%. The keep-pace salary is $60,000 × 1.04 = $62,400, so you are about $600 ahead in real terms. In today’s dollars the new salary is worth roughly $60,577 — a real gain of about $577, not the $3,000 the headline suggests. A 3% raise in that same year would have left you behind.

Raises compound; bonuses do not

This is the reason a smaller recurring raise usually beats a larger one-off payment. A $3,000 raise is $3,000 this year, then it is baked into the base for next year’s raise, and the year after. A $3,000 bonus is $3,000 once. Over five or ten years the gap between the two is large. When you weigh an offer, consider whether the extra is going into base pay or into a bonus that can be cut.

Edge cases and limits

  • Zero raise: enter 0% or $0 to model a pay freeze, then use the inflation check — a frozen salary during 4% inflation is a real cut of nearly 4%.
  • Large raise: there is no maximum, so a promotion or a market-correction jump can be modelled directly.
  • Reverse solving: enter a known flat raise and the equivalent percentage is calculated for you — useful for comparing offers quoted in different formats.
  • Several years of raises: the tool models one raise at a time. For a sequence, run it once per year and feed each new salary back in as the current salary — that is exactly how compounding works.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate a raise?

In percent mode, raise amount equals current salary multiplied by the raise percentage. In flat-amount mode you enter the raise directly. New salary is current salary plus the raise amount.

Percent raise or flat amount — which should I use?

Use whichever your offer is quoted in. The calculator shows the other automatically, so you can compare a “5%” offer and a “$4,000” offer side by side.

Does this show my new salary after tax?

No. It returns gross figures. Run the new salary through the Salary Calculator for a take-home estimate.

How do I know if a raise beats inflation?

Multiply your old salary by (1 + inflation rate) for each year since your last raise. If your new salary is above that keep-pace figure, the raise is a real gain.

What is a “real” raise?

The part of the increase left after inflation. A 3% raise in a year of 4% inflation is a real pay cut of about 1%, even though the number on the letter went up.

Is a 3% raise good?

It depends on inflation and your market. In a low-inflation year 3% is a real gain; in a high-inflation year it can be a real cut. Compare it against your sector’s typical increase as well.

Does a raise compound?

Yes. Next year’s percentage raise is calculated on the higher base, so raises build on each other over a career — unlike a one-off bonus.

What about a promotion with a title change?

Model the salary jump the same way; the calculator does not care why the number changed. Check whether the new role also changes your bonus target or benefits.

Can I model a pay freeze or a cut?

Enter 0 for no change and use the inflation check to see the real erosion. The calculator expects a non-negative raise, so for an actual cut, compare the two salaries directly.
