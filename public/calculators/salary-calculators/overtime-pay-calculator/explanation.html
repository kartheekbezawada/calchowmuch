Extra pay guide

How an overtime pay calculator values the hours you work beyond a normal week

This overtime pay calculator takes your hourly rate, the number of overtime hours, and the multiplier that applies to them, and returns the overtime portion of your pay on its own. Add your base pay for the same period and it also shows the combined gross total. It answers a concrete question: what is one block of extra hours actually worth this week, and is that enough to make the time worthwhile? Because overtime rules differ by country, employer, and shift type, the multiplier is a field you fill in rather than a rule the tool assumes.

Every figure is gross — before income tax and payroll deductions. Overtime pay is taxed at your normal rates when you file; it is not a separate, higher tax band, even though a single large paycheck can push more of that check into a higher withholding bracket for that period.

How to Guide

What this calculator does

It isolates overtime pay from three inputs: hourly rate, overtime hours, and overtime multiplier. Overtime pay equals rate multiplied by hours multiplied by the multiplier. If you also enter base pay for the same period, the calculator adds it on to show total gross pay, so you can see the extra hours both by themselves and as part of the whole paycheck.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your hourly rate — the “regular rate” your overtime is based on.
  2. Add the overtime hours worked in the period.
  3. Enter the multiplier that applies (1.5× for time-and-a-half, 2× for double-time, or the figure in your contract).
  4. Optional: add base pay for the same period to see the combined total.
  5. Select Calculate to see overtime pay and total pay.

How to interpret the results

Result What it tells you
Overtime pay The gross amount those extra hours added, on their own — not the whole paycheck.
Total pay Base pay for the period plus overtime pay, shown only when you enter base pay.
Context line Restates the overtime hours and multiplier the figure is based on, so a saved screenshot is self-explanatory.

Read overtime pay as the marginal value of the extra hours. Divide it by the overtime hours to get the real per-hour rate for that block of work — then compare that against what the time costs you in rest or family time. If the number is small relative to that cost, that is a signal in itself.

Worked example

An hourly rate of $25 with 10 overtime hours at 1.5× (time-and-a-half) produces $375 in overtime pay. Add $1,000 of base pay for the same week and total pay is $1,375. Switch the multiplier to 2× for a holiday shift and the same 10 hours are worth $500, lifting total pay to $1,500.

The regular rate and the multiplier

Overtime is calculated on your “regular rate”, which is not always just your base hourly wage. Shift differentials, non-discretionary bonuses, and some commissions can raise the regular rate an employer must use. If your payslip shows a blended figure, enter that here rather than your headline rate.

Common multipliers: 1.5× for hours over a weekly or daily threshold, 2× for holidays or hours beyond a second threshold, and 1× (straight time) where a jurisdiction or contract does not require a premium. Salaried employees classified as exempt usually receive no overtime premium at all — for them, extra hours are worth zero on this calculator.

Is the extra hour worth it?

Use the effective hourly value to decide. Ten hours at time-and-a-half on a $25 rate is $37.50 an hour; the same hours at straight time are $25. Compare that against a side project, a rested Monday, or simply the marginal utility of more money once your fixed costs are covered.

If overtime is regular rather than occasional, consider whether a higher base rate or a different role would pay more reliably. Overtime does not compound, does not count toward a salary figure on a future offer, and can be cut without notice.

Weekly pay with overtime

To turn this into a full week’s gross pay, set base pay to your regular hours times your rate (for example 40 × $25 = $1,000) and add the overtime block on top. The total pay figure is then your weekly gross before tax. Annualise by multiplying a typical week by your paid weeks per year, but treat that number as a ceiling — few people work the same overtime every week.

Edge cases and limits

  • Zero overtime hours: enter 0 and overtime pay is 0; total pay falls back to base pay. Useful for modelling a normal week.
  • Partial hours: decimals are allowed, so 3.5 overtime hours works.
  • No base pay: leave it blank to see the overtime portion only; total pay is then hidden rather than shown as equal to overtime pay.
  • Very high multiplier: there is no maximum, so you can model a rare triple-time or penalty rate, but check it against your actual agreement first.
  • Multiple overtime tiers: the calculator applies one multiplier at a time. For hours split between 1.5× and 2×, run it once per tier and add the results.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate overtime pay?

Multiply your hourly rate by the overtime hours and by the overtime multiplier. At $25 an hour, 10 hours at 1.5× is $375.

Why does the calculator make me enter the multiplier?

Overtime rules vary by country, state, employer, and shift. Rather than assume one standard the tool cannot verify, it uses the multiplier that matches your situation.

What is time-and-a-half versus double-time?

Time-and-a-half is a 1.5× multiplier, common for hours over a weekly or daily threshold. Double-time is 2×, often used for holidays or hours past a second threshold.

Is overtime taxed at a higher rate?

No. Overtime is taxed at your normal rates when you file. A larger single paycheck can have more tax withheld for that period, but that is a timing effect, not a higher rate.

Do salaried employees get overtime?

Often not. Employees classified as exempt typically receive no overtime premium, so extra hours add nothing on this calculator for them.

What rate should I enter if I get shift bonuses?

Use the blended regular rate, not just your base wage. Shift differentials and non-discretionary bonuses can raise the rate overtime must be calculated on.

Can I get my full weekly pay from this?

Yes. Set base pay to your regular hours times your rate, add the overtime block, and total pay is your weekly gross before tax.

Does the result include tax or deductions?

No. It returns gross overtime and gross total pay. Run the total through the Salary Calculator for a take-home estimate.

Should I rely on overtime for regular budgeting?

Treat it as a cushion, not core income. Overtime can be reduced or removed without notice, so budget from base pay and use overtime to pay down debt or add to savings.
