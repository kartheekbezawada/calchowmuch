How does this debt payoff calculator help?

This page is built for the question people actually ask: when will I be debt-free? Instead of estimating one balance in isolation, it routes your debt payments across every balance you owe, shows how the order you pay them off in changes the outcome, and keeps the answer tied to months, interest cost, and a practical payoff date.

Use it as a debt repayment calculator when you need a real plan, not just a formula. If your situation is only one balance, you can run the one-balance version. If you want to compare structure changes instead of payoff routing, you can test whether debt consolidation helps or check a lower-APR transfer option.

How to Guide

Start by listing every balance you want to pay off. Each row needs a name, a simple debt-type label, the current balance, APR, and the minimum payment. Debt type is descriptive in v1, not mathematical. The engine still uses the same repayment model across credit cards, store balances, medical plans, and personal loans. What changes the outcome is the balance size, interest rate, minimum payment, and the order in which extra money is routed.

After the rows are in place, set the global extra payment pool. This is the amount you can send above the required minimums every month. The calculator adds minimum payments to every active debt first, then routes the extra pool according to the chosen strategy — so the extra money always pays down one target balance instead of being spread thin. That is what makes the strategy comparison real rather than decorative.

  1. Enter all active debts that belong in the plan right now.
  2. Confirm balance, APR, and minimum payment for each row.
  3. Set one extra monthly payment pool that can move to the current target debt.
  4. Choose snowball or avalanche and calculate the plan.
  5. Use goal-date mode when you need to know the payment required to finish by a deadline.

How the monthly math works

Each month begins with interest. The page converts APR into a monthly rate, adds that interest to each active balance, pays every minimum, then routes the shared extra payment pool to the target debt for the chosen strategy.

monthly interest = balance × (APR ÷ 12)

That formula is intentionally plain because the decision is practical: higher APR balances grow faster, so avalanche attacks interest cost first, while snowball attacks the smallest balance first to free a payment slot sooner.

extra payment target = first active debt in strategy order

Minimums still go to every balance. The strategy only decides where the flexible extra amount lands after minimums have been covered.

required payment for goal date = minimum budget + extra amount that solves the target month

Goal-date mode keeps the same payoff engine but searches for the extra payment level that gets the full balance set to zero by the target month.

Snowball vs avalanche

Snowball ranks debts from the smallest balance to the largest. The practical advantage is speed of closure. You remove an entire payment obligation sooner, which can make the plan feel more manageable. Avalanche ranks debts from the highest APR to the lowest. The practical advantage is cost control. More of the monthly budget goes toward balances that would otherwise compound the fastest.

Neither method changes the fact that minimums must still be paid everywhere. The strategy only changes where the flexible money goes after those minimums. If you want to compare the cost of doing nothing beyond minimums, see what minimum-only payments cost. If the debt list actually collapses to one balance, compare with a single-card payoff calculator.

Strategy Best fit Main tradeoff
Snowball Users who want faster visible wins and faster payment simplification May cost more interest than avalanche
Avalanche Users who want the lowest total interest cost May take longer to close the first account

If the real question is not payoff order but whether a structure change beats direct payoff, use compare payoff vs consolidation or compare payoff vs balance transfer.

Which debt should you pay off first?

There are only two defensible answers, and the calculator models both. Pay off the smallest balance first if you want an account closed quickly and one less monthly debt payment to track. Pay off the highest APR first if you want the lowest total interest. Everything else — card issuer, debt type, which statement arrived first — changes nothing about the math.

The practical rule: if the gap between your highest and lowest APR is wide, paying the expensive balance down first saves real money, so choose avalanche. If your rates are close together, the interest difference between the two orders is small and the order that keeps you paying is the better one. Run both and compare the totals rather than guessing — that comparison is the whole point of this page.

One thing does not change with strategy: minimums are still due on every account. Skipping a minimum to accelerate another debt risks late fees and a penalty rate, which costs more than any ordering decision saves.

Should you save money or pay off debt first?

Paying down a balance returns exactly its interest rate, with no market risk. Clearing a card at 24.9% APR removes a known 24.9% annual cost, which is more than the average return most savings or investment accounts deliver — and unlike an investment return, it is not taxed. For high-rate revolving debt, repayment usually wins.

Two things reasonably come before aggressive repayment:

  • A small emergency buffer. Without one, the next unexpected bill goes back onto the card you just paid off, and the plan restarts. Sizing that buffer is what the monthly savings needed calculator is for.
  • An employer retirement match, if you have one. A match is an immediate return on the contribution, which is hard for any interest rate to beat.

Below roughly 6-8% APR the comparison gets genuinely close, and long-run investment growth can come out ahead — you can model that side with the compound interest calculator and weigh it against the interest total this page reports.

The debt payoff formula, and how to calculate it by hand

There is no single closed-form equation for a multi-debt plan, because the extra payment moves to a new target every time a balance closes. What there is, is a repeatable monthly loop. This is the debt repayment formula the engine runs, once per debt, per month:

monthly interest = balance × (APR ÷ 12)
new balance = balance + monthly interest − payment

Repeat until the balance hits zero. The month it does is your payoff date, and the sum of every interest line is your total interest cost.

Worked example: one balance, by hand

Take a $2,000 balance at 22% APR with a $200 monthly payment. The monthly rate is 22% ÷ 12 = 1.8333%.

Month Starting balance Interest added Payment Ending balance
1 $2,000.00 $36.67 $200.00 $1,836.67
2 $1,836.67 $33.67 $200.00 $1,670.34
3 $1,670.34 $30.62 $200.00 $1,500.96

Carried to the end, that balance is repaid in 12 months — the final payment is only $29.80 — at a total interest cost of about $229.80. Notice the interest line shrinking every month: that is why sending extra money early shortens the plan more than sending the same amount later.

With several debts the loop is identical, run in parallel, with one difference: minimums go to every balance, and the extra pool goes to whichever debt sits first in your chosen order. That is the only place strategy enters the arithmetic.

How this differs from a debt management plan

A debt management plan (DMP) is administered by a third party, usually a nonprofit credit counselling agency. You make one monthly payment to the agency, it distributes the money to your creditors, and it may negotiate reduced rates or waived fees on your behalf. Accounts on the plan are typically closed, and the arrangement usually runs three to five years.

This page models the do-it-yourself alternative: you keep control of the accounts, choose your own order, and pay creditors directly. Use the payoff months and total interest here as the benchmark to judge any DMP quote against — if a plan does not beat the number on this page after its fees, it is not buying you much. If the appeal is a single payment rather than rate relief, compare payoff vs consolidation first, since that keeps the accounts in your name.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: One high-rate card drives the plan

When a single credit card drives most of the interest, avalanche often improves the interest result quickly because the extra payment moves straight to the most expensive balance. If the user wants to isolate that debt and see it on its own, the next step is to test one card on its own.

Scenario 2: Minimum-payment trap across several small balances

This is the classic snowball use case. The balances are not individually huge, but the minimums create a drag on monthly cash flow. Snowball can close the first balance sooner and free that payment for the next one. To see how bad the static version becomes, the right pivot is to test the minimum-payment trap.

Scenario 3: Restructure-or-payoff decision

Sometimes the best question is not snowball versus avalanche. It is whether a lower-rate structure would beat both. That is where you should check a consolidation scenario and test whether a transfer saves more. This page stays useful because it gives you the direct-payoff benchmark first.

FAQ

What does debt payoff date mean here?

It is the month when all modeled balances reach zero under the chosen strategy, minimum payments, and extra payment pool.

Does debt type change the math?

No in v1. Debt type is a label for readability. The engine still uses balance, APR, and payment inputs to run the payoff model.

What if I only make minimum payments?

You should see what minimum-only payments cost because that route isolates the downside clearly.

Should I use snowball or avalanche?

Use snowball when quick closures improve follow-through. Use avalanche when minimizing total interest is the main decision goal.

Which debt should I pay off first?

Either the smallest balance, to close an account and free up a payment sooner, or the highest APR, to cut total interest. Run both orders and compare the totals before committing.

Is it better to save money or pay off debt?

Repaying a high-rate balance returns exactly its APR with no market risk, so it usually beats saving. Keep a small emergency buffer and any employer match first.

What is the debt payoff formula?

Monthly interest = balance × (APR ÷ 12), then new balance = balance + interest − payment. Repeat monthly until the balance reaches zero; that month is your payoff date.

What if I only have one balance?

The route still works, but it is cleaner to run the one-balance version.

Can I compare against consolidation?

Yes. Use this page as the direct-payoff benchmark, then compare payoff vs consolidation.

Can a balance transfer beat avalanche?

Sometimes. Use the direct payoff result first, then check a lower-APR transfer option.

Does the calculator include new spending?

No. The model assumes no new debt is added after the plan starts.

How does goal-date mode work?

It estimates the total monthly payment needed to clear the balances by the target month using the chosen strategy order.

Why are there charts and a schedule table?

The charts show the overall path, while the schedule table shows the month-by-month or year-by-year mechanics that support the answer.
